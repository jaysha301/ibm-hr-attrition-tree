## 1. Drop-in paragraph: why overtime could matter (for "Why It Matters and What to Do"; identical to v6)

Research on real workplaces gives reasons overtime could matter, and reasons for care. Long hours can drain time and energy from family life (Greenhaus & Beutell, 1985), and that kind of conflict is linked to leaving (Rubenstein et al., 2018). Equity theory holds that people who feel they give more than they get may leave to restore the balance (Adams, 1965); this data does not record how people felt. But research measures workload, not overtime, and finds it only weakly related to leaving, if anything slightly protective (Rubenstein et al., 2018). Demands like workload generally go with lower turnover; obstacles like red tape and unclear roles go with higher turnover (Podsakoff et al., 2007). Research on workload would not lead us to expect a gap this large, so treat this fictional result as a pointer to where to look, not proof of cause or evidence about any real employer. This data does not record whether overtime was chosen or required, or how long it lasted, and in a real company those are the first things to find out.

## 2. Pay and career stage (context item; identical to v6)

Lower-paid staff (roughly the bottom third, under about $3,000–$4,000 a month) left at about 27%, against 11% for everyone else, wherever the pay line is drawn in that range. But 95% of them are in the most junior job level, so this data cannot say whether pay or career stage matters. Most of their extra leaving is among those who also work overtime: without overtime, lower-paid staff leave at about the company average (16%), against 8% for better-paid staff without overtime. Research finds that both lower pay and being newer to a company go with more leaving (Rubenstein et al., 2018), so treat this as one picture, not two separate explanations.

(Only the last sentence is research text; the rest is the cleared exec-page context.)

## 3. Suggestions to test (aimed at overtime workers as a whole group; not proven fixes)

Four suggestions to test, not proven fixes. Each applies to teams and to the overtime group as a whole, never to individuals and never selected by age, gender or marital status.

1. **Review workload and staffing where overtime is concentrated.** Map overtime by team and role, and ask whether it is a predictable crunch or a chronic pattern, and whether it is chosen or required. Where it is heavy, sustained and not chosen, start with demands that get in the way (red tape, unclear roles, understaffing) rather than work that stretches people.
   *Why:* obstacles at work go with higher turnover while stretching demands do not (Podsakoff et al., 2007), and long hours can crowd out family life (Greenhaus & Beutell, 1985).
2. **Offer manager check-ins ("stay conversations") to everyone in the overtime group.** Ask what keeps people and what might make them leave, then act on what is in the team's control. Offer them to the whole group, never to people picked by personal characteristics.
   *Why:* ties to colleagues, fit with the job and what people would give up by leaving help explain why people stay (Mitchell et al., 2001), and people's reasons for staying differ by job type (Hausknecht et al., 2009).
3. **Review pay and recognition for overtime effort across the overtime group.** Check whether overtime is paid or recognized, and whether raises are handed out by a clear, fair process. Note that pay and career stage cannot be separated in this data.
   *Why:* Classic theory holds that people stay while what they get outweighs what they give (March & Simon, 1958), and that feeling under-rewarded can prompt people to leave (Adams, 1965).
4. **Pick one measure and pilot before rolling out.** Track the leaving rate of overtime workers by team, side by side with overtime levels. Try changes in a few teams first and compare them with similar teams that did not change, before rolling out more widely.
   *Why:* Research finds that most single factors are only modestly related to leaving (Griffeth et al., 2000; Rubenstein et al., 2018), and this data is fictional, so only a company's own tracked results can show whether a change helps.

## 4. Check of the wording "overtime is usually only a weak predictor (Rubenstein 2018)"

**Verdict: inaccurate as written.** Rubenstein, Eberly, Lee & Mitchell (2018, *Personnel Psychology*, 71, 23–65) meta-analysed **workload**, defined as "mostly measures of hours worked or how hard and fast an individual works" (Table 1), not an overtime yes/no flag. Workload was weakly and slightly protectively related to leaving (rho = −.10, k = 21). Work–life conflict was linked to more leaving (rho = +.19, k = 7). Overtime is only a stand-in for workload here, so the claim must be about workload, not overtime.

**Status in v5/v6:** this sentence was already replaced in v4 (Quinn's fix R1, `analysis/qa_log.md`) and is unchanged in v5 and v6. The wording is accurate. Keep the v6 paragraph in section 1. Exact wording for the sentence:

> But research measures workload, not overtime, and finds it only weakly related to leaving, if anything slightly protective (Rubenstein et al., 2018).

**Podsakoff, LePine & LePine (2007):** workload and time pressure are classed as "challenge" demands, which were generally linked to **lower** turnover. Obstacles such as red tape and unclear roles ("hindrance" demands) were linked to higher turnover. So the text must never call overtime inherently harmful, and whether overtime here felt like an obstacle is unmeasured. Keep the "challenge/hindrance" labels out of executive text; describe them in plain words as above.

## 6. Do not say

- **Causal language:** "overtime causes leaving", "cutting overtime will reduce attrition", "low pay drives people out". Say "goes with", "is linked to", "who left, not why".
- **Forecast language:** "will save 60 leavers", "attrition will drop to 12%". The 16% → 12% figure is an illustration of scale, not a forecast.
- **Importance percentages or model scores** (e.g., "overtime explains X%", "the top driver").
- **Targeting by age, gender or marital status**, including "young single overtime workers" or offering check-ins to chosen demographic groups.
- **Pay and career stage as separable:** never "pay, not seniority, matters" or "pay matters even after career stage".
- **"Starting where overtime meets the lowest pay"** or any pointer at the overtime-by-low-pay combination in the cleared exec-page text (Quinn F2: small-group highlight in disguise).
- **"Younger" or age wording** in the pay/career-stage research sentence (Quinn F1); use pay and "newer to a company".
- **"Far bigger than research would predict"**: research makes no prediction about an overtime flag; say it "would not lead us to expect a gap this large" (Quinn F3).
- **"Drivers"** for factors linked to leaving (causal; Quinn F6). Say "factors".
- **Citing Rubenstein for gender.** Their equal-employment quote covers "age, marital status, or how many children they have" only. The gender rule is this team's own.
- **"Overtime is a weak predictor (Rubenstein)"**: Rubenstein measured workload, not overtime.
- **"Overtime is harmful" / "a hindrance"**: research classes workload as a demand linked to lower turnover.
- **"The data confirm the research"**: fictional data cannot confirm or refute research.
- **Jargon:** cross-validation, CI/confidence interval, kappa, rho/ρ, AUC, lift, bootstrap, p-values, test set, "challenge/hindrance stressor", embeddedness, LMX.
- **Any statistic not on the cleared page**, including the 55%/19% overtime-by-pay split (appendix only).

**Greenhaus & Beutell (1985): theory only, no leaving claim.**
- Crossref and Semantic Scholar hold no abstract for this DOI (publisher elided). I did **not** read the paper's full text today (closed access, no open copy found).
- What it rests on: the published abstract reproduced on ResearchGate ("time devoted to the requirements of one role makes it difficult to fulfill requirements of another ... strain from participation in one role makes it difficult to fulfill requirements of another ... specific behaviors ... A model of work-family conflict is proposed"), and the standard definition quoted widely by later papers (a form of interrole conflict in which the pressures of work and family are mutually incompatible, p. 77).
