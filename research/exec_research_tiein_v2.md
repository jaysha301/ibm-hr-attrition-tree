## Item 1. Overtime workers (cleared finding)

**Numbers (page):** 416 people, 28% of staff; 31% left vs 10% without overtime; 54% of everyone who left. As an illustration, not a forecast: if they left at the company average, overall attrition would fall from about 16% to about 12%, roughly 60 fewer leavers.

**Why this could matter in real organizations** *(126 words)*
Research on real workplaces gives reasons overtime could matter, and reasons for care. Long hours can drain time and energy from family life (Greenhaus & Beutell, 1985), and that kind of conflict is linked to leaving (Rubenstein et al., 2018). Equity theory holds that people who feel they give more than they get may leave to restore the balance (Adams, 1965); this data does not record how people felt. But research measures workload, not overtime, and finds it only weakly related to leaving, if anything slightly protective (Rubenstein et al., 2018). Research on workload would not lead us to expect a gap this large, so treat this fictional result as a pointer to where to look, not proof of cause or evidence about any real employer.

**Suggestions to test** (teams and the overtime group as a whole; not proven fixes)
1. **Review workload and staffing where overtime is concentrated.** Map overtime by team and role, and ask whether it is a predictable crunch or a chronic pattern, and whether it is chosen or required.
   *Why:* Obstacles at work, such as red tape and unclear roles, go with higher turnover while stretching demands do not (Podsakoff et al., 2007).
2. **Offer manager check-ins ("stay conversations") to everyone in the overtime group.** Ask what keeps people and what might make them leave, then act on what is in the team's control.
   *Why:* Ties to colleagues, fit with the job and what people would give up by leaving help explain why people stay (Mitchell et al., 2001), and reasons for staying differ by job type (Hausknecht et al., 2009).
3. **Pick one measure and pilot before rolling out.** Track the leaving rate of overtime workers by team, try changes in a few teams, and compare with similar teams that did not change.
   *Why:* Research finds that most single factors are only modestly related to leaving (Griffeth et al., 2000; Rubenstein et al., 2018), so only a company's own tracked results can show whether a change helps.

## Item 2. Within overtime: lower-paid, mostly junior workers (part of the overtime finding, not a separate pattern)

**Numbers (page):** overtime workers earning under roughly $3,000 to $3,500 a month (no exact cut; about 115 to 130 people, 8% to 9% of staff) left at about 55%, and account for close to 30% of everyone who left. 97% are in the most junior job level. Overtime workers in the most junior job level (156 people) left at 53%, against 17% for the 260 overtime workers at higher job levels (company average 16%). Illustration only: if they left at the company average, attrition would fall from about 16% to about 13%, roughly 45 to 50 fewer leavers. These people are already inside the overtime group, so this overlaps item 1 and is not added to it.

**Why this could matter in real organizations** *(126 words)*
Pay and career stage move together here, so this is one picture and the data cannot say which matters. Classic theory holds that people stay while what they get outweighs what they give (March & Simon, 1958), and that feeling under-rewarded can prompt people to leave (Adams, 1965); this data does not record how people felt about their pay or their overtime. Research finds that lower pay goes with more leaving, and so does being newer to a company, though both links are modest (Rubenstein et al., 2018). People early in their time with a company have had less chance to build the ties, fit and stake that research links to staying (Mitchell et al., 2001). This is consistent with the pattern, as an illustration only.

**Suggestions to test** (aimed at junior-level overtime workers as a job-level group, never by pay cut, age, gender or marital status; not proven fixes)
1. **Check overtime pay, recognition and scheduling at the junior job level.** Ask whether overtime is paid or recognized, whether notice is given, and whether extra hours are shared fairly across the team.
   *Why:* People weigh what they give against what they get (March & Simon, 1958; Adams, 1965).
2. **Hold a defined next-step conversation for junior roles.** Make clear what the next level involves and how people get there, offered to everyone at that level.
   *Why:* Seeing fit with the organization and a stake in staying go with staying (Mitchell et al., 2001).
3. **Pilot in a few junior-level overtime teams, with measurement.** Track their leaving rate against similar teams that did not change before rolling out.
   *Why:* Single factors are only modestly related to leaving in research (Rubenstein et al., 2018), so local results decide.

## Item 3. WATCH ITEM, not a cleared finding: new hires and short tenure

**Numbers (page):** across the company, staff with 1 year or less at the company (215 people, 15% of staff) left at 35%, about 40 people above the company rate (2.7 points); about a third (32%) work overtime. It held up on the employees set aside for checking (44%), but the exact group is not stable when the analysis is repeated, because new hires, short tenure, junior level and low pay overlap. So the whole career-stage cluster is one picture to watch, not a proven separate cause, and it is not added to the overtime finding. Without overtime, staff with 1 year or less (146 people) left at 25%, against 8% for those without overtime who have been there longer. The page's wording: "That is a real difference, but staff without overtime leave less often than the company overall, so against the company average it is modest."

**Why this could matter in real organizations** *(128 words)*
Research finds that longer time with a company goes with less leaving (Rubenstein et al., 2018); people newer to a company have had less chance to build the ties, fit and stake that go with staying (Mitchell et al., 2001). Research on newcomers finds that how people are brought in matters: structured orientation, finding the information they need, a clear role and feeling accepted by colleagues relate to how well new hires settle, and settling relates to outcomes that include intending to stay and actually staying (Bauer et al., 2007). Here, new hires, short tenure, junior level and low pay overlap, so treat them as one picture to watch, not a proven cause. Without overtime the gap is real in this group but modest against the company rate.

**Look into** (watch item; groups of roles and teams, never personal characteristics)
1. **Review onboarding for the first year across roles and teams.** Check whether new hires get a clear role, the information they need and a chance to meet colleagues.
   *Why:* Orientation, information and role clarity relate to newcomer adjustment (Bauer et al., 2007).
2. **Hold a 90-day and a 6-month check-in for all new hires.** The same offer to everyone who joins.
   *Why:* Early ties and fit relate to staying (Mitchell et al., 2001); newcomer adjustment relates to intending to stay (Bauer et al., 2007).
3. **Track first-year leaving by team.** Report it next to overtime levels, so leaders can see whether it is a company-wide pattern or sits in a few teams.
   *Why:* Single factors are only modestly related to leaving in research (Rubenstein et al., 2018), so a company's own tracking is the test.

## Do not say
- **Causal language:** "overtime causes leaving", "low pay drives people out", "short tenure leads to leaving", "onboarding will fix". Say "goes with", "is linked to", "who left, not why".
- **Forecast language:** "will save 60 (or 45 to 50) leavers". The impacts are illustrations of scale. **Never add** the item 1 and item 2 illustrations, and never add item 3 to either.
- **Item 2 as a third pattern or a pay target.** It is part of the overtime finding. Aim suggestions at junior-level overtime workers as a job-level group, not at "people under $X" (there is no exact cut) and not at a pay-cut group.
- **Item 3 as a finding.** It is a watch item. Never "proven", "confirmed" or "the second driver". Never call the exact group (1 year or less) stable.
- **Pay and career stage as separable:** never "pay, not seniority, matters".
- **"Far bigger than research would predict"** or "the data confirm the research". Say research "would not lead us to expect a gap this large".
- **"Overtime is harmful" / "a hindrance"**: research classes workload as a demand linked to lower turnover.
- **Targeting by age, gender or marital status**, including "young", "single", "newly married". Cite Rubenstein's equal-employment quote (age, marital status, number of children) only for those; gender is this team's own rule.
- **Importance percentages or model scores** ("overtime explains X%", "top driver").
- **Small-group highlights:** no groups under 100 people; no exact pay-cut figure.
- **Jargon:** cross-validation, CI, kappa, rho/ρ, AUC, lift, bootstrap, p-values, test set, "challenge/hindrance stressor", embeddedness, mediation, socialization tactics (say "how people are brought in").
- **Statistics not on the exec page.** The rho figures stay out of executive text.
